## Before We Begin

A distribution of 100,000 won lands in your account. It's the kind of welcome notification you look at twice, even over coffee. But if your cash balance rises by 100,000 won, did your investment grow by that amount? **Separate the reason cash arrived from the reason your total assets changed.**
<br/>
Money a company pays to shareholders is a dividend. Money a fund such as an ETF pays to investors is a distribution. This article focuses on fund distributions. We'll make **a worksheet for what your cash is for and when you'll use it next**.

<Rbox>
The calculations illustrate the concepts and are not investment recommendations. The PortRhythm screenshot uses a test account and may show different figures.
</Rbox>

## Distributions and Total Return

When an ETF pays cash out of its holdings, that amount leaves the fund. The SEC's investor guide explains that a distribution reduces net asset value and transfers that value to investors, for example as cash. **The amount deposited in your account is only part of your investment result.**
[Investor.gov on fund distributions](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/fund-distributions-investor-bulletin)
<br/>
Assume prices otherwise stay the same and there are no fees or taxes. An equity ETF you hold pays 100,000 won. Start with the [10 million won portfolio from Part 1](https://www.promleeblog.com/blog/post/458-portfolio-reading-consolidated-holdings).

| Category | Before the distribution | After keeping it in the account |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Stocks | 6 million won | 5.9 million won |
| Bonds and gold | 2.5 million won | 2.5 million won |
| Investment cash | 1.5 million won | 1.6 million won |
| Total | **10 million won** | **10 million won** |

The 100,000 won moved from stocks to cash. **Total return** considers both changes in value and the money you received.
<br/>
A large distribution doesn't mean you earned an equally large profit. A fund may distribute cash while losing money, and some payments may include a return of capital. Check how distributions are funded and paid before treating an estimate as dependable income.
[Investor.gov on distributions and performance](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/fund-distributions-investor-bulletin)

## Three Ways to Use the Same 100,000 Won

You can keep the money in the account, use it to buy more assets, or withdraw it to pay living expenses. Here's what the **same 100,000 won** looks like in each case.

<Img src="/portfolio-reading-07-distributions-cash-20261017.webp" width={750} height={500} alt="How keeping, reinvesting, or withdrawing a 100,000 won distribution changes the account balance"/>

| Choice | Stocks | Cash | Total in the account | Record separately |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Keep it in cash | 5.9 million won | 1.6 million won | 10 million won | Cash increased inside the account |
| Reinvest 100,000 won | 6 million won | 1.5 million won | 10 million won | Purchase inside the account |
| Withdraw it for living expenses | 5.9 million won | 1.5 million won | 9.9 million won | External withdrawal of 100,000 won |

Bonds and gold stay at 2.5 million won in every case. The reinvestment buys more of the same equity ETF. If you withdraw the cash, distinguish the 9.9 million won in the account from the 100,000 won you took out. Keep withdrawn money out of the current investment balance.
<br/>
Using [the method from Part 4](https://www.promleeblog.com/blog/post/461-portfolio-reading-return-cash-flows), investment profit or loss in the withdrawal case is `990만 − 1,000만 − (−10만) = 0원` (9.9 million minus 10 million minus a negative 100,000 won). The balance fell because of the withdrawal. Cash generated by an investment and kept in the account should also stay separate from money you contributed from outside.

## Give Your Cash a Purpose

When you see 1.5 million won in cash, you might wonder whether to invest all of it. Start by recording **when and where you'll use the money**. Give cash with different purposes separate labels.

| Cash category | Purpose | Scope in this series |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Living expenses | Upcoming day-to-day spending | Outside the investment total |
| Emergency savings | Unexpected costs or an interruption in income | Outside the investment total |
| Investment cash | Future investments, settlement, or allocation changes | Inside the investment total |

Investor.gov's guide explains why it's useful to set aside money for unexpected events. Choose an amount based on your living expenses, income stability, and upcoming spending.
[Investor.gov on emergency savings](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/save-and-invest/save-rainy-day)
<br/>
The 1.5 million won here is **investment cash kept separate from living expenses and emergency savings**. Also check that you haven't counted the same money as both a brokerage cash balance and a separate cash asset.

## Check Your Allocation Before Reinvesting

You can buy more of the original ETF with every distribution. First, look at your current weights. If you're already above your stock target, consider using the cash for an underweight asset or keeping it in cash. That connects the distribution to **bringing your allocations back toward target**.
<br/>
If you'll need the money soon, think before investing it in a hurry for fear of missing gains. Cash may miss price gains and lose purchasing power to inflation, but it can keep money available when you need it. **How easily you can turn an asset into spendable money** is called liquidity. The practical question is whether you can use the money on the day you need it.

<Bbox>
Give the cash a specific condition for use. For example, “Check which assets are underweight at the next review before investing.” That takes some pressure off deciding whether to spend it all today.
</Bbox>

## A Cash Flow Planning Worksheet

Here's how you could label the 1.5 million won in investment cash before receiving the distribution.

| Purpose | Amount set aside | When to review or use it |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Next investment | 700,000 won | Decide after the next monthly review |
| Settlement reserve | 500,000 won | Reclassify after checking orders and settlement records |
| Allocation adjustments | 300,000 won | Check the gap from target and how much cash you need |
| Total | **1.5 million won** | |

When the 100,000 won arrives, move it from expected to confirmed cash and decide which purpose it serves. Avoid adding a distribution already included in the balance. If it was automatically reinvested, reconcile the extra shares as well as the cash.

## Reconcile Cash and Records in PortRhythm

In the asset editor, check the cash name, amount, and currency. Under asset assignment, check which port holds it. See the [asset registration guide](https://www.promleeblog.com/blog/post/438-portrhythm-asset-registration-guide) for details. Reconcile the entries with your broker's distribution records and cash balance.

<Img src="/portrhythm-assets-desktop-cash-20261008.webp" width={750} height={422} alt="Asset editor showing the cash name, amount, currency, and assigned port"/>

Verify the name, amount, currency, and assigned port. Reconcile won and dollar cash in their respective currencies. Check separately how much you can actually spend or withdraw from the account.
<br/>
When money leaves the investment scope, record an external withdrawal in asset history. Distributions kept in the account and purchases within it are separate from external cash flows. See the [asset history guide](https://www.promleeblog.com/blog/post/443-portrhythm-asset-history-guide).
**Account for the distribution, balance change, and external withdrawal once each.**

## Conclusion

A 100,000 won distribution is welcome, but one notification can't tell you your overall return. Decide whether to keep, reinvest, or withdraw it, and record what the cash is for and when you'll use it. **The worksheet helps you explain the role of the cash you hold.**
